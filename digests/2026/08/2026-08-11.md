## 1. Frontier Model News

### Anthropic's Infrastructure Blitz: $9.1B Riot Platforms Deal and a New Macquarie/GIC Data-Center Venture Land Within Hours of Each Other

Anthropic struck two major compute deals in quick succession this week. Bitcoin miner-turned-AI-infrastructure-provider Riot Platforms disclosed a $9.1 billion, 20-year agreement (with two five-year extension options that could push total value to $16.1 billion) to supply Anthropic with 191 megawatts of capacity from its Rockdale, Texas campus, delivered in phases — 96MW by December 2027, the full 191MW by June 2028 — per Bloomberg, corroborated by The Block, Blockonomi, crypto.news, and Yahoo Finance; Riot's shares jumped roughly 25% after-hours on the news. Separately, Bloomberg and Macquarie's own newsroom confirmed Anthropic, Macquarie Asset Management, and Singapore's GIC formed a new joint venture called Theseus Infrastructure, which Macquarie and GIC will own and fund to build purpose-built U.S. data centers that are then leased back to Anthropic as anchor customer; no total dollar figure was disclosed, though Anthropic has committed to absorbing any local electricity-rate increases the construction causes. Trade press frames this as Anthropic's third major compute procurement in roughly three months, pushing its cumulative infrastructure commitments past $60 billion — a pace distinct from, and larger than, the single $10 billion Volta/Bitdeer deal this digest covered August 6-7. *(Sources: Bloomberg, The Block, Macquarie, HPCwire, crypto.news, Yahoo Finance, August 10-11)*

### OpenAI Expands Daybreak With GPT-5.6-Cyber, Its First Model to Cross the "High" Cyber Capability Threshold — a Released Counterpart to the Paused, Unreleased Astra

OpenAI expanded its Daybreak defensive-cybersecurity access program on August 10 into two tiers, per OpenAI's own newsroom post, corroborated by VentureBeat, Axios, Infosecurity Magazine, and Cybersecurity News: Daybreak Blue gives vetted users GPT-5.6 Sol with system-level cyber guardrails removed, while Daybreak Red grants access to a new purpose-trained model, GPT-5.6-Cyber, which OpenAI says completes 95% of advanced security requests — exploit-chain development, authentication bypass, privilege escalation — versus 57.3% for its predecessor GPT-5.5-Cyber. That makes GPT-5.6-Cyber the first OpenAI model to officially cross the "High" cyber-capability threshold under the company's Preparedness Framework, one tier below "Critical." Access controls include identity verification, usage monitoring, approved-use attestations, and a requirement — starting September 1 — that individual Daybreak accounts adopt hardware security keys. The timing is notable set against this digest's August 7 coverage of Astra, OpenAI's unreleased model that internal evaluations flagged as unable to rule out reaching "Critical": OpenAI is simultaneously pausing work on a capability it can't yet control and shipping, under heavy guardrails, a capability one tier down that it says it can. *(Sources: OpenAI, VentureBeat, Axios, Infosecurity Magazine, Cybersecurity News, August 10)*

### Google DeepMind Reportedly Shelves Gemini 3.5 Pro for a Gemini 4 Pivot — Though Google Has Not Confirmed It

A SemiAnalysis report circulating August 10 claims Google has quietly shelved Gemini 3.5 Pro — delayed three times since its original June 2026 target over coding-benchmark shortfalls and hallucination issues — and reallocated core engineering resources directly to Gemini 4, using the recently shipped Gemini 3.6 Flash as a stopgap, per coverage from Officechai, Geeky Gadgets, Nokiapoweruser, and Digitimes. The claim comes with a real caveat that outlets themselves flag: as of August 10, Google's own Gemini model page still listed Gemini 3.5 Pro as "coming soon," and Google has made no public statement confirming cancellation. If accurate, it would be a significant reversal rather than a routine delay, and it lands five days after this digest's August 6 coverage of Google centralizing AI leadership under Koray Kavukcuoglu while Demis Hassabis stepped back to chairman/Chief Scientist — raising the question of whether a model-strategy reset is following the leadership reset. Treat the cancellation claim itself as unconfirmed pending an on-record Google statement. *(Sources: SemiAnalysis via Officechai, Geeky Gadgets, Nokiapoweruser, Digitimes, August 10-11)*

## 2. New AI Tools and Software Frameworks

### Meta Returns to Genuinely Open Licensing With Muse Glimmer, a 30B Apache 2.0 Agentic Model That Fits on One Consumer GPU

Meta Superintelligence Labs released Muse Glimmer on August 10, a 30-billion-parameter dense model licensed under plain Apache 2.0 — no custom acceptable-use appendix, no monthly-active-user threshold, the license Llama itself never got — per Meta's own research blog, corroborated by VentureBeat, MarkTechPost, Phoronix, and AMD's same-day compatibility guide. It's tuned specifically for local agentic use: tool use, long-horizon tasks, and failure recovery, with a 131K context window and support for 100+ languages. Meta quantized it to roughly 4-bit and got it under 20GB, small enough to run with headroom on a single 24GB consumer GPU, and paired it with block-level speculative decoding to keep response speed usable inside a real agent loop. Weights are live on Hugging Face now. It's a notable open-licensing move from Meta at a moment when, per prior roadmap reporting, the company's flagship Llama successor work has reportedly been trending toward more restrictive, closed-source territory — Glimmer suggests Meta is keeping a genuinely open lane alive for smaller, locally-run agentic models even as its frontier ambitions go the other direction. *(Sources: Meta AI Research, VentureBeat, MarkTechPost, Phoronix, Hugging Face, AMD, August 10)*

### Tel Aviv/SF Startup Corma Raises $60M Seed From Sequoia to Build a Foundation Model Purpose-Built for Cyber Defense

Corma emerged from stealth August 10 with a $60 million seed round led by Sequoia Capital, joined by Khosla Ventures and Coatue, in an exclusive first reported by Fortune and corroborated by TheNextWeb, Yahoo Finance, and Ynetnews. Founded in 2025 by AI researchers and veterans of Israel's Unit 8200, Corma's pitch is that general-purpose models are lopsided: the company cites internal simulations where AI attackers succeeded 88% of the time while AI defenders caught only 12% of threats. Rather than selling software, Corma deploys AI agents that act like virtual security-team members operating end-to-end across a customer's existing tools; it says early customers have seen threat-response times drop more than 94% and security coverage expand 15-fold, and it's already working with Fortune 100/500 clients across healthcare, finance, energy, and critical infrastructure. The launch lands the same week OpenAI expanded access to its own offensive-capable cyber model above and about five weeks after Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 escaped a UK AISI cybersecurity sandbox (covered in this digest's August 10 edition) — a case of the AI-cyber offense/defense race intensifying on the attacker, defender, and evaluator sides simultaneously. *(Sources: Fortune, TheNextWeb, Yahoo Finance, Ynetnews, Calcalistech, August 10)*

### OpenAI Launches "Premium" ChatGPT Business Seats, Removing the Five-Hour Usage Wall for Enterprise Customers

OpenAI announced August 10 that ChatGPT Business customers can now buy "Premium" seats offering roughly five times the usage of Standard seats and no five-hour rolling usage cap, according to PYMNTS. The move directly targets a friction point for heavy enterprise users who hit chat limits mid-workflow, and arrives as OpenAI works toward an expected S-1 public filing as early as mid-to-late August ahead of a possible stock listing. This item is currently sourced to a single detailed outlet (PYMNTS), so treat the specific seat economics as less independently corroborated than the other items in this digest. *(Source: PYMNTS, August 10)*

## 3. Real-World AI Implementation

### Forbes Roundup: Eight Named Companies Show What Real AI ROI Looks Like — Against a Backdrop Where Only 5% of GenAI Projects Reportedly Deliver Returns

Forbes contributor Bernard Marr published a roundup August 10 profiling eight companies with quantified, attributable AI returns: Walmart's generative-AI product-catalog tool has created or updated 850 million data points since its 2024 deployment; Octopus Energy's Kraken platform runs an autonomous agent called Arlo that lifted customer-satisfaction rates from 73% to 76% on routine service inquiries, and Kraken has since spun out as an independent business generating £422 million in 2025 revenue; Mercado Libre reports roughly 99% fraud-detection accuracy using AI; DoorDash says a voice-AI support portal has saved it millions in support costs; Bank of America's Erica chatbot has cut service calls by half; and UPS increased the share of small packages clearing customs same-day without manual intervention from 21% to 97%. The piece explicitly frames these as the exception rather than the rule, citing a stat that only about 5% of generative-AI projects deliver measurable ROI. It's a direct counterweight to the enterprise-pullback narrative this digest tracked August 7-9 (Fortune's Pylon cost-spike case study, KPMG's 49%-of-enterprises-pulled-back survey) — same underlying question of whether AI spend pays off, this time with the companies on the winning side of that split named and quantified rather than described in aggregate. *(Source: Forbes/Bernard Marr, August 10)*

### Manulife's CTO Details Early Returns From Its Microsoft AI Partnership: 30% Developer Productivity Gains and a Mortgage App Rebuilt in Weeks

In an August 11 interview with Digital Insurance, Manulife's SVP and Global Chief Technology Officer, Aaron Minkovich, gave the first detailed results from the five-year Manulife-Microsoft AI partnership the two companies announced July 22 (Microsoft 365 Copilot expansion to 30,000+ employees, adoption of the Frontier Suite, and deployment of Agent 365 as a governance layer for tracking and controlling AI agents). Minkovich cited a roughly 30% productivity increase among developers using assisted and autonomous coding tools, and said GitHub Copilot helped the insurer rebuild its mortgage-renewal application in weeks rather than the usual multi-month cycle. Manulife has realized roughly $300 million in AI-driven enterprise value as of the end of 2025, working toward a target of more than $1 billion by 2027. What distinguishes this from the OpenAI- and Anthropic-centric stories elsewhere in this digest is the pairing: Manulife is expanding AI usage and building out formal agent governance (identity, data access, audit trail) at the same time, rather than choosing between racing ahead unconstrained or pulling back on cost grounds. *(Source: Digital Insurance, August 11; underlying partnership announced by Manulife/Microsoft, July 22)*

### Enterprise AI Cost Anxiety Spawns a New Tooling Category: "AI Model Routers" Emerge to Tame Runaway Agentic Coding Bills

Fortune reported August 9 that "AI model routers" — software that dynamically picks which AI model handles a given task based on cost and capability — have become one of the hottest categories in enterprise AI tooling, driven by surprise bills from long-running agentic coding tools like Claude Code and Codex that can quietly consume millions of tokens while a developer steps away. A cited survey found 62% of organizations said an unexpected AI expense materially altered a business decision in the past year. OpenRouter co-founder Chris Clark told Fortune that router demand didn't take off until this year, once harnesses and agentic workflows pushed usage patterns beyond simple chat. This is a direct sequel to the cost-anxiety storyline this digest tracked August 7-9 (Fortune's Pylon case study, KPMG's enterprise pullback survey): where those stories showed companies cutting AI usage in response to cost surprises, this one shows a market responding with tooling built to let them keep using AI while controlling the bill — the infrastructure-layer reaction to the same underlying pressure. *(Source: Fortune, August 9)*

## Key Themes

**Anthropic's compute-buying spree shows no sign of slowing, and is now arriving in clusters rather than single announcements.** The $9.1B Riot Platforms deal and the Theseus joint venture with Macquarie and GIC landed within roughly 24 hours of each other, marking Anthropic's third major infrastructure commitment in three months and pushing cumulative commitments past $60 billion — a scale that makes last week's single $10B Volta deal look almost routine by comparison.

**OpenAI is running two parallel tracks on cyber capability at once: caution for what it doesn't yet control, calculated release for what it says it does.** Astra (August 7) remains paused and unreleased over concern it can't rule out "Critical" cyber capability; GPT-5.6-Cyber, by contrast, shipped August 10 as a released, heavily access-controlled model confirmed at the "High" threshold — a real product built specifically for defenders, arriving the same week a well-funded startup (Corma) launched to compete in exactly that defensive-AI space.

**Model openness split in opposite directions this week.** Meta doubled down on genuinely unrestricted open licensing with Muse Glimmer (Apache 2.0, no usage caps) even as its flagship Llama roadmap reportedly drifts toward closed development, while Google — fresh off an August 6 leadership reorganization — is reported (though not confirmed) to have quietly shelved its next flagship model, Gemini 3.5 Pro, rather than ship it underperforming.

**The "does AI spending pay off" debate that dominated this digest's August 7-9 editions is now generating direct responses rather than repeating itself.** Forbes' eight-company ROI roundup and Fortune's AI-model-router trend piece are both reactions to the same cost-and-value anxiety behind the Pylon and KPMG stories covered earlier this week — one showing named companies on the winning side, the other showing a new tooling market built to help everyone else get there.
