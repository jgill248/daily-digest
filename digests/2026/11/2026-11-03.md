## 1. Frontier Model News

### Anthropic's amended S-1 shows $16.96 billion of third-quarter revenue, a $567 million operating loss and $895.4 billion of purchase commitments, and warns it is approaching its recursive self-improvement threshold
Anthropic filed an amended registration statement on November 2 that fills in the blanks left by the October 26 S-1 this digest covered last week. Per CNBC, which read the filing first, revenue for the nine months to September 30 was $33.24 billion, with $16.96 billion of that in the third quarter, up about 47% on the second quarter and roughly 1,100% year on year. The quarter swung to an operating loss of $567 million from a $974 million operating profit in the second quarter, which the filing attributes mainly to about $5.0 billion of short-term compute bought at higher rates than long-term arrangements. Net loss for the quarter was $23.29 billion and for nine months about $104 billion, more than $94 billion of it non-cash remeasurement of convertible notes and derivative liabilities rather than operating cost. The commitments table, blank in the first filing, now totals $895.4 billion, "substantially all" for compute and infrastructure, with about $100.2 billion entered into after quarter end. TechCrunch focused on a new risk factor: the company says it is nearing its internal threshold for recursive self-improvement under its Responsible Scaling Policy, that no current model has crossed it, that it has already used AI to build models whose capability gains it did not recognise at the time, and that using AI to build more capable AI "could also pose a considerable safety risk" if it accelerates progress faster than safety work. The price range remains blank. *(Sources: CNBC, TechCrunch, Quartz, Dataconomy, ppc.land, November 2–3)*

### OpenAI hires former Army Secretary Dan Driscoll as senior counselor for national security modernization, and more than quadruples its top bug bounty to $172,000 with Cathedral Systems joining its next security sprint
Reuters reported on November 2 that OpenAI has appointed Dan Driscoll, who resigned as US Secretary of the Army earlier this year after months of reported tension with Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, to a newly created post advising leadership and working with outside partners on how advanced AI can strengthen national security and cybersecurity. CNBC and Seeking Alpha carry the same "senior counselor" title; Gizmodo notes Driscoll is a longtime friend of Vice President Vance from Yale Law School. No compensation or reporting line was disclosed. Separately, OpenAI said in a post on X the same day that it is planning its next internal security sprint and bringing in Cathedral Systems, whose researchers will use OpenAI's models alongside OpenAI's own teams to attack its defences, and that it is more than quadrupling rewards for critical findings in its Security Bug Bounty: a critical flaw in ChatGPT, Codex or the API Platform now earns up to $30,000, and exceptional findings up to $172,000 including bonuses, with the new ranges applied retroactively to awards from the past 30 days. OpenAI has not said when the sprint starts or what access Cathedral gets. Cathedral, founded by four former DOGE staffers, is the firm whose agents reached a limited set of Anthropic's sales, HR and IT systems through third-party software in late October; Anthropic said the test overstated its reach and did not fully follow its bug-bounty rules, and Cathedral disputes that. *(Sources: Reuters, CNBC, Gizmodo, OpenAI on X, Interesting Engineering, Quartz, November 2–3)*

### Musk says Grok Bot's serving cost was "unsustainably high relative to revenue" as subscribers hit weekly caps early, while xAI launches Data Bot and sets API watermarking for November 7
Subscribers to Grok Bot, xAI's agent product bundled into the $30, $100 and $200 tiers of the X subscription, reported over the weekend that weekly usage allowances were running out in hours; one quoted by TechSpot said a meter went from 56% to 100% in an hour, and The Decoder, citing the Telegraph, says several users saw quotas roughly halved. Replying to a complaint on X on November 1, Elon Musk wrote that "the cost of serving Grok Bot was unsustainably high relative to revenue,". Forbes notes the admission came less than two weeks after Musk called Grok Bot the fastest-growing product he had seen on SpaceX's third-quarter call, where company figures put paid weekly users at roughly 800,000 in early October. Neither Musk nor xAI has disclosed cost figures, confirmed a change to the caps or published the per-tier allowance as a hard number, and some Cursor users reported usage readings spiking without extra activity, so part of the effect may be metering rather than policy. The same day xAI launched Data Bot, a shared Team Bot that lets any employee query the data warehouse using skills the data team maintains, with memory additions gated by a manager's approval; xAI says it absorbed about 70 hours of data-science work in its first week. And xAI's API documentation confirms that from November 7 text outputs from Grok 4.3 through 4.7 carry an invisible statistical watermark by default, with per-model opt-out in the console. *(Sources: TechCrunch, Forbes, TechSpot, The Decoder, Teslarati, xAI, November 1–3)*

### Nvidia tells cloud partners to sell at least 51% of their capacity to customers outside China, the Financial Times reports
The Financial Times reported on November 3, citing five people familiar with the matter, that Nvidia has told data-centre and cloud providers that use its chips to ensure at least 51% of their services go to non-Chinese customers. Per France 24, The Next Web and Techzine's accounts of the report, the requirement applies to new contracts rather than existing leases, was delivered verbally through sales teams rather than in writing, and does not bar Chinese customers outright. The target is the workaround that has grown up since US export controls: Chinese AI firms renting Nvidia capacity in Singapore, Malaysia and elsewhere over remote connections, so that no hardware crosses a border. A Singapore-based provider told the FT it is "difficult to find enough non-Chinese customers outside the US" and that strict enforcement would hurt its business; others worry they would have to scale back builds. Nvidia neither confirmed nor denied the figure, saying instead that "America cannot win the AI race unless America's AI infrastructure wins worldwide" and that US policy encourages providers to build on American technology. A Chinese foreign ministry spokeswoman said she was not aware of the rule. The move lands six days after Nvidia called allegations that it diverted chips to China "investigated and debunked," and as Washington weighs its own crackdown on offshore Chinese compute, which CNBC reported in August. *(Sources: Financial Times via Bloomberg, France 24, The Next Web, Techzine, Benzinga, November 3)*

## 2. New AI Tools and Software Frameworks

### Anthropic opens message threads in public beta on the Messages API and extends its text watermark to four older Claude models from November 3
Anthropic's developer account announced on November 2 that message threads are in public beta on the Messages API: the server keeps the conversation between turns, so a client sends only new messages plus the previous message ID instead of the full history, and Claude "starts responding sooner." It works on the Claude Platform, on AWS, in Microsoft Foundry and on Google Cloud at standard token rates. Per The Next Web and a developer write-up, Anthropic reports median time to first token about 35% lower on 500,000 to one-million-token requests, but the full conversation is still processed each turn, so there is no token or price saving. One comparison table says threads live in memory for five minutes and are excluded from zero-data-retention and HIPAA configurations; that detail rests on a single source and Anthropic's docs could not be opened from this session. Threads are already on by default for most Claude Code users. Separately, an email to Claude Platform customers summarised by The Decoder and Yahoo Tech says the invisible SynthID-style text watermark that newer models already carry extends to Mythos 5, Opus 4.7, Opus 4.6 and Sonnet 4.6 from November 3, with Amazon Bedrock a few days later and no opt-out. For Opus 4.6 and Sonnet 4.6 the email says the mark applies only at default temperature, top_p and top_k; the two newer models carry no such caveat. *(Sources: Anthropic on X, The Next Web, The Decoder via Yahoo Tech, DEV Community, November 2–3)*

### Trajectory opens its post-training platform to the public with a $45 million Sequoia-led Series A at a $300 million valuation
TechCrunch reported on November 2 that Trajectory, an 18-person San Francisco startup founded about a year ago by Stanford classmates Ronak Malde, Michael Elabd and Arjun Karanam, has raised a $45 million Series A led by Sequoia with Nvidia, Bessemer Venture Partners and Bain Capital Ventures participating, valuing the company at $300 million, and has opened its platform to the public the same day. Malde was among the Windsurf researchers Google DeepMind hired, Elabd also worked at DeepMind and Karanam worked on generative foundation models at Apple. The product targets the gap between a fine-tuning API and a research team: a company starts from an open-weight model, connects Trajectory to its production application, and the platform collects feedback on how the model performs inside that application and uses it to retrain the model for the specialised task, with the stated aim of letting domain experts rather than ML engineers drive training. Harvey, Rogo, Decagon, Mercor and Nvidia are named as customers. TechCrunch lists Thinking Machines Lab's Tinker, Applied Compute and Prime Intellect as the competitors Trajectory itself cites. Two details conflict in secondary coverage: one outlet calls the round a Series B, and a venture tracker records a $40 million Series A in August at the same valuation, which may be the same round at an earlier stage; the TechCrunch account is the one used here. *(Sources: TechCrunch, Digg, Runtime Wire, TokenPost, November 2–3)*

## 3. Real-World AI Implementation

### Marvell agrees to buy Lumentum for about $115 billion in stock and Oracle raises $20 billion, as the AI build-out's bill moves to optics and the bond market
Marvell and Lumentum announced on November 2 an all-stock merger with an implied enterprise value of about $115 billion, the largest semiconductor deal on record by that measure. Lumentum holders get 4.5 Marvell shares each, worth about $1,132 per share and a 23.9% premium to the October 28 close before deal talk began; they will own about 33% of the combined company, and the companies expect to close in the first half of 2028 after Lumentum shareholder, HSR and other approvals; Marvell owes $3.2 billion if regulators block it. The pitch is that optical interconnect is the next bottleneck as AI clusters scale; the market was unconvinced, with Marvell closing down 12.7% at $171.41 and Lumentum the worst performer in the S&P 500. TD Cowen cut Marvell to Hold, seeing 2027 transceiver demand at $47 billion against a roughly $100 billion consensus, while Raymond James upgraded Lumentum and called the reaction overdone. The same morning Oracle said it would raise $20 billion, half in 86.2 million shares priced at $116, an 11.2% discount, and half in bonds maturing 2029 to 2046 at reported coupons of 7% to 8.65%, and pledged that this is all the outside AI financing it will raise through fiscal 2030. Oracle fell 8.8% to $119.22, extending its 2026 loss to about 39%, and Alphabet launched a €6 billion seven-tranche euro bond the same day. *(Sources: Lumentum 8-K via SEC, CNBC, Reuters via The Globe and Mail, Benzinga, Investing.com, Business Standard, GuruFocus, November 2–3)*

### Goldman Sachs pulls Claude Code and GitHub Copilot from traders and bankers, and Wells Fargo makes customers liable for whatever their AI agents do from November 24
Bloomberg reported on November 2 that Goldman Sachs has restricted Claude Code and GitHub's coding tools to its roughly 12,000 engineers under internal guidelines updated in October, after a review of productivity and risk concerns, cutting off traders and bankers who had for months used them to track client flows, simulate market and position shifts and draft text and visuals; the "strats" who straddle trading and engineering are reportedly exempt. The reversal is sharp because Chief Information Officer Marco Argenti spoke favourably in July of "vibe coding" by non-technical staff. A spokesperson said the bank makes "certain AI tools available to our people to determine the best use cases across different parts of the firm," and Bloomberg did not say which findings drove the change. On the retail side, American Banker reports that Wells Fargo's updated Online Access Agreement, effective November 24, adds a section under which any instruction or transaction initiated by an AI agent a customer has authorised "is deemed to have been initiated and authorized directly by you, without further verification," makes customers responsible for revoking that authority and asks them to keep a record of when they did so, because the bank "may not otherwise have visibility." The clause applies "if and when" the bank permits agents at all, which it has not yet said. It follows last week's blocks by Canada's five largest banks and American Banker's count of JPMorgan, Citi, Bank of America and Morgan Stanley already blocking outside agents. *(Sources: Bloomberg via Yahoo Finance, Quartz, Finextra, PYMNTS, American Banker, TechTimes, November 2–3)*

### The New York Times and three Tribune-owned papers sue Brave over a 30-billion-page index sold to Anthropic, Perplexity, Mistral, xAI and Cohere
The New York Times filed a copyright suit against Brave Software in the Southern District of New York on November 2, and the Daily News, Chicago Tribune and Orlando Sentinel filed a parallel complaint the same day, per TechCrunch, Bloomberg Law and Search Engine Journal. Brave is the only defendant. The Times alleges Brave built an index of more than 30 billion pages with an unidentified crawler that publishers cannot block, resells that material through Brave Search, Ask Brave and the Brave Search API to AI companies that use it to train models and ground answers, and names Anthropic, Perplexity, Mistral, xAI and Cohere as customers of that service. It also attacks the browser itself, saying it lets users around the Times paywall, strips Times advertising and substitutes Brave's own, and that the Leo assistant summarises articles; the complaint cites cease-and-desist letters in August 2025 and April 2026 that it says drew no substantive response, and seeks an injunction and damages. Perplexity's communications head Jesse Dwyer called the claims "disinformation"; Brave had not responded by publication. The suit arrives four days after the four largest US labs agreed to honour a single robots.txt opt-out for training, and it targets the loophole that agreement leaves: an intermediary crawler whose index is sold on. *(Sources: TechCrunch, Bloomberg Law, Search Engine Journal, The Verge, Dataconomy, November 2–3)*

## Key Themes

**The bill is now itemised.** Anthropic's $895.4 billion of commitments, Oracle's $20 billion raised at a discount and 7% to 8.65% coupons, Alphabet's second euro bond of the year and Marvell's $115 billion bet on optics all landed within a day, and the market's answer was uniform: every issuer and acquirer fell. Capital is still available for the build-out, but as of this week it is priced, capped and voted on, and the recipients have started pledging limits on how much more they will ask for.

**Serving cost is the constraint behind every product decision.** Anthropic's operating profit vanished into $5 billion of spot compute; Musk throttled his fastest-growing product because serving it cost more than it earned; Anthropic's message threads buy latency but explicitly no token savings; and Goldman decided the productivity case for frontier coding agents did not hold for 30,000 non-engineers. The year's arguments were about capability. This week's are about unit economics.

**Boundaries are being written in contracts, not model cards.** Nvidia set a 51% rule for who may rent its chips, Wells Fargo wrote into its terms who bears the loss when an agent misbehaves, the Times is suing the intermediary that sits outside the labs' voluntary robots.txt pact, and xAI and Anthropic are both switching on watermarks that mark the output rather than restrict it. Governance of agents and models is arriving as commercial terms between private parties, well ahead of regulation.

**Anthropic put the self-improvement question into a securities filing.** Disclosing that models have already produced capability gains the company did not recognise at the time, and that the recursive self-improvement threshold is near, converts a safety-policy debate into a risk factor investors will price. Whether that is caution or marketing, as one commentator asked, it is now a statement the SEC can hold the company to.
