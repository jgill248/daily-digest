## 1. Frontier Model News

### An Anthropic mathematician says he and Claude proved the Riemann Hypothesis, and the Lean community certified the formal proof a day later
Levent Alpöge, a number theorist employed by Anthropic, posted a four-page note titled "Remark" on his personal website on Friday, November 20, saying that over roughly six weeks of work with Claude he had found a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis and of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for all Dirichlet L-functions. The proof exists only as Lean code: 175,187 modules and about 17 million lines in 14 archives, with no human-readable argument, which Alpöge says would be "enormous" and which he expects "is likely to be significantly simplified, potentially quite soon." Coverage does not name the Claude model used. On Saturday the Lean and Mathlib communities, backed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, issued a joint statement certifying that the formal statement is exactly Mathlib's Riemann Hypothesis and that the proof compiles under Lean and Mathlib 4.33.1. MIT's Andrew Sutherland published a dashboard showing all modules compiling with zero errors, a separate kernel replay accepted every module, and Epoch's Elliot Glazer confirmed the formalized statement is GRH. Reaction split sharply. Fields medallist James Maynard said that "if this fully checks out, it is clearly a historic milestone," Terence Tao offered only a preliminary read that the strategy "appears to use the full power of modern developments on the Langlands program and arithmetic geometry," and Stanford's Brian Conrad told the NYT that releasing a proof its author does not fully understand is "frankly completely outrageous." Hours after Alpöge's post, AMS president Ravi Vakil published a "hurried announcement" of a roughly 940-page, 12-year, "all human, with no AI" draft proof of GRH by Stanford's Alessandro Masullo, which Conrad has been vetting for months. Alpöge told NPR he asked Dario Amodei on November 15 whether he could announce and acknowledged the timing, one day after Anthropic's Nasdaq debut, could be read cynically. The Clay Mathematics Institute still lists the problem as open; its rules require peer-reviewed publication and a two-year wait. *(Sources: NPR, AFP, Nature, Science, TechCrunch, The Decoder, Lean community blog, November 20–23)*

### OpenAI's chief scientist published the company's pretraining loss curve and proposed that labs agree to shared perplexity limits
Jakub Pachocki posted an essay titled "Perplexity and Intelligence" on OpenAI's site on Sunday, November 22, disclosing for the first time internal log-perplexity figures on a held-out internal codebase: about 0.99 for GPT-3.5, about 0.91 for GPT-4, and below 0.8 for OpenAI's latest models. He wrote that he expects transformative capabilities to arrive somewhere in a window of roughly 0.68 to 0.70, while saying he "does not know what those capabilities will be," and warned that AI could begin accelerating pretraining research itself "as soon as 2027." His proposal is that frontier labs agree on common limits, based on perplexity and other measures, that none would cross until the industry's ability to understand and monitor model behaviour reaches a concrete milestone. The post names no numeric cap, no auditor and no signatories, and it is voluntary. Critics noted within hours that the metric is scored on a private test set only OpenAI can run, and that reinforcement learning, tools and scaffolding can add capability without lowering perplexity, a limit Pachocki himself concedes when he writes that perplexity "does not directly measure performance on a task people care about." Sam Altman reposted the essay, calling it "the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while." The proposal lands two months after Dario Amodei's September 12 essay "We Must Pace the Frontier," which Altman and Musk endorsed, and five days after OpenAI signed embedded third-party evaluators. *(Sources: TechCrunch, The Next Web, TechTimes, Crypto Briefing, November 22–23)*

### Anthropic closed its second day at $88.91 as the Nasdaq posted its worst week since 2020, then AI stocks rebounded in Monday's premarket
Anthropic fell 8.5% on Friday to $88.91, about 7% above its $83 offer price and 20% below Thursday's $111.50 open, as the Nasdaq dropped 5.3% on the day and about 10% on the week, its worst since the early pandemic weeks of 2020, wiping out its gains for the year. The S&P 500 and Dow both closed in correction territory, Tokyo, Seoul and Taipei fell between 5% and 6.2%, and Nvidia's 14% Friday plunge, covered below, led the index down. The same day Anthropic said the offering had closed: 1.127 billion Class A shares at $83 including the fully exercised 147 million-share option, for gross proceeds of about $93.5 billion, of which Anthropic receives about $91.6 billion, plus 313.25 million Class N shares sold at $83 to AMD, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, Nvidia and TSMC Partners for about $26.0 billion, or roughly $119.5 billion in all before fees. NPR ran a piece on Saturday asking whether public ownership will test the company's stated willingness to put safety ahead of growth. On Monday morning Nasdaq-100 futures were up roughly 3%, Nvidia rose 8% to 9% premarket, AMD and Broadcom gained close to 6%, Micron about 7%, and Anthropic traded 3% to 4.5% higher depending on the hour. Strategists quoted by Reuters and Yahoo Finance called the bounce an oversold reflex after seven straight weekly declines for Nvidia and said the Nasdaq remained about 18% below its peak ahead of Wednesday's PCE inflation print. *(Sources: CNBC, CNN, Reuters, Anthropic press release, NPR, Yahoo Finance, November 20–23)*

### Nvidia fell 14% on a Reuters report of an SEC accounting probe, as Bessent demanded answers and the NYT reported Alice Huang was in contact with China's spy agency
Reuters reported on Friday, citing two people briefed on the matter, that SEC enforcement staff are scrutinising potential accounting issues at Nvidia. Reuters could not determine when the inquiry began or what it covers; PYMNTS linked it to 2025 changes in how Nvidia reports revenue by geography, which Reuters did not confirm, and it is unclear whether it is the same civil probe into Asia-related disclosures the WSJ reported last week. Shares closed down 14.01% at $125.53, a 23% loss on the week and more than 40% in a month, and Trump the same day said Huang should make a "big apology" and return as CEO, which the board has ruled out. On Sunday's "Face the Nation," Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said "we do need answers" on how chips reached China, that officials have been in touch with Nvidia's interim management, lead directors and lawyers, and that he wants to know who oversaw compliance and whether knowledge reached "all the way to the top." He called the focus on Alice Huang "kind of a canard," said Nvidia had not yet answered the ten-day request that expires November 25, and declined to endorse Huang's return. Also on Sunday the New York Times reported, citing people with knowledge of intelligence reports, that Alice Huang was in contact with an officer of China's Ministry of State Security from spring 2025 into autumn 2026, overlapping Megaspeed's purchases of advanced Nvidia chips. The Times said the reporting does not establish that Jensen Huang was spied on or that Nvidia information reached Beijing, and the attorney general and DNI both said no conclusion has been reached. A companion Times analysis traced about $3.6 billion in Chinese state funding through companies tied to her family and associates, including a cybersecurity venture co-owned with an arm of China Electronics Technology Group. Nvidia and the Chinese embassy did not comment. Huang's closed-door House interview remains set for December 1. *(Sources: Reuters, Bloomberg, CBS News, New York Times via Reuters, PYMNTS, November 20–22)*

### A database maintenance job took down ChatGPT and the OpenAI API for 3.5 hours on Saturday, with failure rates peaking at 75%
OpenAI's status page says requests to the API and to ChatGPT failed between 1:48 p.m. and 5:21 p.m. Pacific on Saturday, November 21, with the failure rate peaking at 75% across paid and free ChatGPT tiers and across Chat Completions, Embeddings, Images, Batch, Audio, Moderations, Realtime, Fine-tuning and Files. ChatGPT login, search, file upload and voice mode were all affected. The post-incident note, quoted by several outlets, says the outage "was triggered by maintenance on a PostgresDB service used by OpenAI's Primary API service that caused both read replicas to fail at the same time." Because the Primary API handles authentication for the rest of the stack, every dependent request path failed with the replicas. Attempts to revive either replica and to bring up replacements in other regions did not work, and the incident was mitigated only by working with the database provider to bring two new replicas online in the same region, 211 minutes after it began. OpenAI says it has paused similar maintenance, adjusted connection settings and added a feature flag to shift traffic away from replicas. The outage came a day after OpenAI published its Performance Factory post on Codex agents diagnosing ChatGPT slowdowns, and in the same week that banks and the NSA reportedly blamed rushed patches for other outages; nothing in the status note connects this incident to either. *(Sources: OpenAI status page via AI Weekly, Emergent, DEV Community, November 21–22)*

## 2. New AI Tools and Software Frameworks

### Google opened Genie 4 to paid Gemini API developers at about $0.10 per second of generated world, with sessions capped at 120 seconds
Genie 4, the interactive world model DeepMind previewed to trusted testers on October 28, became available through the Gemini API and Google AI Studio on November 19 as model ID genie-4 on the v1beta Interactions API. Pricing is $1.50 per million tokens for text or reference-image input, $9 per million for text output and $17.50 per million video output tokens with stereo audio included, which works out to roughly $0.10 per second or about $6 per minute of 720p, 24 frames-per-second video; a maximal 120-second session costs about $12 in video charges before input. There is no free tier, and Batch, Flex, Priority and context caching are not offered. Third-party testers report visual stability degrading after 60 to 90 seconds, consistent with DeepMind's own warning in October that longer sessions drift. Coverage conflicts on status, with Releasebot's changelog summary calling it generally available and others calling it a preview; the Interactions API it rides on is itself beta. Google's pricing page had not listed Genie 4 as of the weekend, so the per-second figures are derived from the per-token rate in the release notes. *(Sources: Gemini API release notes via Releasebot, ppc.land, Unite.ai, DataStudios, November 19–22)*

### Anthropic put Claude computer use into beta for Team and Enterprise plans, off by default and with a warning that there is "no sandbox"
Anthropic's November 20 release notes add computer use, which lets Claude see a user's screen and click and type in permitted desktop apps, as a beta for Team and Enterprise organisations, "rolling out gradually." It stays off until an organisation owner enables it, works only in the unified Claude experience on Claude Desktop, web and mobile when the organisation's Computer access setting is on, and does not work in tasks started with the Cowork option or in Claude Code. Owners can restrict Claude to an app allowlist, Enterprise admins can gate it by custom role, a compliance policy can disable it with no user override, and IT can block it per machine; macOS 14 or later is required. The Help Center article states that "computer use has no sandbox between Claude and your applications," that Claude can open, change or delete files outside connected folders through approved apps, and that Cowork's deletion protection does not apply. Browsers and trading or crypto apps are view-only, terminals and code editors allow clicks but not typing, and Anthropic recommends against granting access to banking, healthcare or government apps. The same day's notes also begin a redesign of the Claude iOS app. The release comes three days after Google gave Gemini for Mac computer use and a week after Apple split macOS agent permissions. Separately, the Claude-BugHunter skill bundle that topped GitHub trending on Friday was still first on Monday at 18,819 stars, up 2,037 in a day. *(Sources: Anthropic release notes, Claude Help Center, GitHub trending, November 20–23)*

### OpenRouter dropped three inference providers and 125 endpoints after Dragos seeded two million canary prompts and watched the secrets get used
HD Moore, chief research officer at Dragos, sent about two million prompts to AI providers between July and November, each carrying a unique never-used bait credential such as an AWS access key, an SSH password or a private git link, and then watched where each one surfaced. Ars Technica reports that the canaries revealed at least three inference providers whose systems had been breached by attackers siphoning customer prompts, resellers that logged prompts they said they did not keep, and providers that ignored zero-data-retention flags. At one breached provider attackers reused "a couple hundred" of the AWS keys, some within hours of seeding. A separate finding was that a Chinese AI lab's systems fetched the secret URLs embedded in more than half the prompts sent to its models between July and October. None of the providers knew until the researchers told them. On November 22 OpenRouter said it had off-boarded three providers and removed 125 endpoints from zero-data-retention eligibility, including some as a precaution, while stating that its own platform was not breached; it has not named the three. Together AI said the leaked samples came from prompts to MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2 and Kimi K2.6 on specific dates in June and July, and Atlas Cloud, Novita, Phala and Together have published notices. Anyone who sent keys through affected endpoints is being told to rotate them. *(Sources: Ars Technica, The Next Web, OpenRouter, Notebookcheck, November 21–22)*

### OpenAI released MedChemBench, scoring GPT-6.2 Astra at 84.5% on medicinal chemistry reasoning while conceding the test is near saturation
OpenAI published MedChemBench on November 20, a benchmark of 99 problems drawn from 117 papers and patents covering 761 molecular structures across structure reading, structure-activity relationships, property reasoning, lead optimisation and retrosynthesis. At its xhigh reasoning setting GPT-6.2 Astra earned 84.5% of available credit, against 83.3% for GPT-6 Astra, 82.0% for GPT-6.1 Sol and 58.2% for GPT-5.6 Sol. The gains are uneven: models score about 95% on reconstructing structures from text but only about half the credit on ranking candidates and selecting compounds. OpenAI states the results do not establish that models can discover drugs or improve laboratory decisions, says the benchmark is close to saturation, and acknowledges that the source papers may appear in training data. It is an internal evaluation with no public dataset, so the scores cannot be reproduced outside OpenAI, and no Claude or Gemini numbers were published. Some secondary writeups quote scores near 49% for GPT-6 Astra, which appear to come from a different scoring method and could not be reconciled. *(Sources: OpenAI, Unite.ai, Crypto Briefing, November 20–22)*

## 3. Real-World AI Implementation

### Signal now blocks screenshots by default on Android and iOS to stop OS-level agents reading chats, and calls them an "existential threat"
Signal president Meredith Whittaker wrote on November 20 that AI agents built into operating systems are "an existential threat to Signal," and the app began turning on screen protection automatically. On Android, Signal 8.35.1 or later detects an AI assistant attempting to read the screen and switches on the existing Screen security flag, which Signal says does not work reliably on every Android variant. On iOS 27 with Signal 8.37 or later a new "Block Siri AI" setting is on by default. The named targets are Apple's server-side Siri AI and Google's Gemini. Signal says the fix is incomplete: agents can still reach application data through the device filesystem, Android accessibility permissions act as a "skeleton key," and Signal cannot offer equivalent protection on macOS as it did against Microsoft Recall on Windows. The company asked Apple and Google to let developers opt their apps' screens, notifications and files out of AI processing entirely. The move follows Reuters' report last week that Meta's Muse pulled Signal messages from Android notifications and Mac accessibility tools, and lands in the same week Google and Anthropic shipped desktop computer use to more users. *(Sources: Signal blog, TechCrunch, The Hacker News, Notebookcheck, Digital Trends, November 20–22)*

### Walmart switched on its Agent Gateway with Meta's Muse as the first shopping agent, in time for Black Friday
Walmart's Agent Gateway, announced October 21 as a Model Context Protocol server for AI agents, went live over the weekend with Meta's Muse as the first consumer agent, letting Muse search Black Friday deals, build carts and complete purchases on Walmart.com. The first time Muse adds an item to a cart it sends the shopper to Walmart to sign in and create an Agent Profile with a spending limit, after which purchases are charged to a card already saved in the Walmart account at the same prices shown on Walmart.com and under the normal return policy. Shoppers can require approval for each purchase or let Muse spend up to the cap, review everything it has bought, and the agent never sees card details or can change payment methods; Walmart has not said whether limits apply per order, per day or per month. Walmart says the gateway finds products without sharing a customer's purchase history and that Muse is "just the beginning," with other agents to follow. The contrast with Amazon, which blocked Muse checkout on September 20 and has not restored it, is the point: Walmart is betting that governed agent access wins the Black Friday week that runs through November 29. One source also names Sam's Club as covered; others do not. *(Sources: The Verge, Walmart, Digital Commerce 360, The Next Web, November 22–23)*

### Tesla now has more robotaxis registered in Texas than Waymo, 1,507 to 1,364, though Waymo still leads on vehicles in service and miles
Texas Department of Motor Vehicles registry data as of November 22 shows Tesla with 1,507 vehicles authorised for driverless operation in the state, split between 773 purpose-built Cybercabs and 734 Model Ys, against 1,364 for Waymo, out of roughly 3,700 automated vehicles on the registry. Tesla first edged ahead on November 10 at 1,302 to 1,297 and widened the gap when it registered a batch of 200 Cybercabs on November 17; TechCrunch's Mobility newsletter quotes Waymo at 1,345, a few days older. The registry counts authorisations, not vehicles giving rides, and on that measure Waymo remains well ahead, with more than 4,000 driverless vehicles nationally against 666 for Tesla per Robotaxi Tracker figures cited by Electrek on November 9, and a lead in commercial driverless miles that TechCrunch notes Tesla has not closed. Tesla said on its Q3 call that Cybercab rides would launch in Dallas this month and that it aims to run Robotaxi around the clock by year end; neither has been independently confirmed. *(Sources: TechCrunch, EVwire, Crypto Briefing, Electrek, November 22)*

## Key Themes

**Machine-checked but not understood.** The weekend's biggest story is also its strangest: a Millennium Prize problem may have fallen to a proof that compiles but that nobody, including its author, can yet explain. The Lean certification settles whether the code proves Mathlib's statement; it does not settle whether mathematicians will accept a 17-million-line artifact as knowledge, and the same-day arrival of a 940-page human draft sharpens that question rather than resolving it.

**The labs are now proposing their own brakes.** Pachocki's perplexity essay, coming after Amodei's pacing plan and the embedded-evaluator deals, means OpenAI and Anthropic have both put voluntary limits on the table within a quarter. Each proposal is scored on data only the proposer can see, and none names an enforcer, so for now they are statements of intent that a Democratic Congress, state attorneys general and the SEC will test from outside.

**The market priced the week as a referendum on AI and voted no, then reconsidered.** A record $119.5 billion raise coincided with the Nasdaq's worst week since 2020, an SEC accounting probe at Nvidia and intelligence-report leaks about the Huang affair. Monday's futures bounce says positioning, not conviction, drove Friday; the November 25 Treasury deadline and December 1 testimony will decide whether Nvidia's problems are a governance story or a disclosure story.

**Agents reached the screen and the checkout, and the defences are catching up piecemeal.** Anthropic and Google now ship desktop computer use with explicit "no sandbox" language, Signal is blocking screenshots because the operating systems will not, Walmart is governing agents with spending caps while Amazon still blocks them, and a security researcher found that the inference layer underneath all of it was leaking prompts for months. The pattern across every story is the same: access first, controls second.
